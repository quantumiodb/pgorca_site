A Cascades optimizer is a search engine built around one question: *which
of these equivalent plans is cheapest?* ORCA can enumerate bushy join
orders, decorrelated subqueries and pushed-down aggregates, but every one of
those choices is settled by a number the cost model returns. If that number
is wrong, the search will find the wrong plan very efficiently.

ORCA came out of Greenplum with a cost model calibrated for MPP clusters.
Its constants are bandwidths and unit costs measured on Greenplum hardware
(`DSeqIOBandwidth = 1024`, `DTableScanCostUnit = 5.5e-07`, plus send and
receive costs for every kind of Motion). On a single PostgreSQL node it
priced plans in a different unit from PostgreSQL. It also ignored the knobs
DBAs already tune (`random_page_cost`, `work_mem`,
`effective_cache_size`), and comparing EXPLAINs could not tell you why ORCA
had picked a plan.

So [`pg_orca`](https://github.com/quantumiodb/pgorca) ships a second cost
model, `CCostModelPG`, which ports PostgreSQL's `costsize.c` into ORCA one
operator at a time. It has been the default (`pg_orca.cost_model = pg`)
since May 2026.

ORCA already hides costing behind an `ICostModel` interface. During search,
the engine calls `Cost(exprhdl, costing_info)` for every candidate physical
expression in the Memo, passing the children's total costs, row counts,
widths and rebind counts. `COptTasks::GetCostModel` picks the implementation
from the GUC.

`CCostModelPG::Cost` keeps PostgreSQL's additive structure: the sum of the
children's costs plus a local cost for the operator. It dispatches to two
dozen helpers such as `CostScan`, `CostIndexScan`, `CostHashJoin`,
`CostNLJoin`, `CostHashAgg`, `CostSort` and `CostLimit`. It does not keep
its own copy of the cost constants. It declares PostgreSQL's planner globals
`extern "C"` and reads them live, so `SET random_page_cost = 1.1` shifts
ORCA's choice between index scan and seq scan just as it shifts
PostgreSQL's.

ORCA models `WHERE unique1 < 7000` as a Filter operator above the scan,
while PostgreSQL attaches the qual to the scan node. `CostScan` charges
`cost_seqscan`'s page I/O and per-tuple CPU (445.00). `CostFilter` charges
one `cpu_operator_cost` per qual operator per input row (25.00). The sum is
the 470.00 that PostgreSQL prints.

The larger operators bring more of PostgreSQL's detail with them. Hash
joins separate hash clauses from residual quals and take bucket sizes from
histograms. HashAgg models spilling with `hash_agg_entry_size` against
`work_mem × hash_mem_multiplier`. Sort derives its merge order from
`work_mem`, and btree scans get the real tree height through
`amgettreeheight`.

## Where the two models disagree

The formulas were the easy part. The hard part is that ORCA and PostgreSQL
describe a plan differently. Three gaps come up again and again in the 53
commits on `CCostModelPG.cpp`.

**No startup cost.** PostgreSQL tracks `startup_cost` and `total_cost`
separately, and `LIMIT` needs both: `startup + (total − startup) ×
fraction`. ORCA reports only a child's total. `CostLimit` estimates the run
portion from the type of the child: a Sort's cost is almost all startup,
and an IndexScan's is almost none.

**Rebinds instead of loop counts.** ORCA prices the inner side of a nested
loop by multiplying it by the number of rebinds. PostgreSQL's `cost_index`
uses `loop_count` instead, and spreads heap I/O across repeated probes with
the Mackert–Lohman formula, because later probes find pages already cached.
`CostIndexScan` passes ORCA's rebind count in as `loop_count`.

**Early termination.** A semi or anti join stops at the first match.
Following `final_cost_nestloop`, `CostNLJoin` charges each matched outer
row only `2 / (match_count + 1)` of an inner scan. Without this, an `EXISTS`
in the style of TPC-H Q4, with an indexed inner, came out about 3× too
expensive. That pushed ORCA toward hashing the whole inner side and
deduplicating it.

A cost model that is "PG-aligned" only in its comments drifts quickly.
`test/cost_align.sh` runs 311 EXPLAINs twice, once through PostgreSQL's
planner and once through ORCA with `cost_model = pg`. When both pick the
same top operator, their costs must agree within 2%. Today 183 of those 188
plans do. Commit messages for cost changes routinely quote the before and
after counts.

Aligning the costs also changed which plans ORCA was willing to pick.
PostgreSQL prices a CTE Scan very cheaply. Once ORCA used the same price, it
began choosing a Merge Join over two CTE Scans, a plan the Greenplum
calibration had always priced out. PostgreSQL's CTE Scan can't mark and
restore its position, which a merge join's inner side needs, so that plan
crashed. The fix belonged in ORCA's property derivation rather than in the
cost model, and it shows the general lesson: a new cost model opens new
regions of the search space, and the tests have to follow.

## Why it matters

When both planners price plans the same way, a `pg_orca` EXPLAIN reads like
a PostgreSQL EXPLAIN. A cost of 470 means the same thing in both, and your
tuning carries over. When ORCA still picks a different plan, the cause is a
different search space or a different row estimate, not a different
exchange rate, and that makes the plan much easier to debug.
